Sushi breakfast · Tsukiji 築地 vs Toyosu 豊洲 · verified 2 August 2026

Sushi Dai at Tsukiji? Two restaurants wear that name. Here’s where to actually eat.

This isn’t ordinary stale information — it’s a collision. The famous 5 a.m. queue counter Sushi Dai moved to Toyosu in October 2018. A genuinely separate, separately-managed restaurant called Tsukiji Sushi Dai Honkan still trades at Tsukiji — opens 10:30, takes cards, earned its own Tabelog Top-100 nod. Half the internet has merged them into one imaginary restaurant. This page keeps them apart, explains the slot queue that replaced the line, and maps where dawn sushi actually exists in 2026.

TL;DR

  • The famous Sushi Dai is at Toyosu (Block 6, fish building, 3F) and has been since October 2018. Omakase ¥5,500, cash only, same-day timed slots from a storefront desk at 05:00 — you take a slip and come back. ¥4,500 is a pre-2023 price.
  • Tsukiji Sushi Dai Honkan is a different business that happens to share the name: 10:30 open, dinner to 22:00, 40 seats, VISA and Mastercard, its own Tabelog Sushi TOKYO Top-100 (2021) selection. Going there at dawn expecting the queue shop — or queueing at Toyosu expecting cards — are both collision casualties.
  • Dawn nigiri at Tsukiji barely exists. Most named Tsukiji sushi restaurants open 10:00–11:00. The honest dawn plays are kaisendon at Kanno from 04:30 or the 24-hour, 144-seat Sushizanmai Honten — details in chapter 04.

The name collision, side by side

Three forces compound here. The 2018 move left thousands of well-ranking pages pointing at the wrong market. A real restaurant with an almost identical name kept trading at Tsukiji. And even the Tsukiji association’s own shop page still lists the Honkan’s pre-2020 hours (“10:30 to 5:00 the following morning” — a specialist Tsukiji food guide and the restaurant’s own listing both confirm the close was cut back to 22:00 during COVID and never restored). So a diligent reader checking an official source can still walk away wrong. Here is the untangled version:

A Tsukiji food writer with over 1,000 market visits states it flatly: the super-queue Sushi Dai from the inner market (now Toyosu) is separately managed from Tsukiji Sushi Dai Honkan. Major travel platforms still merge them — one OTA publishes a post titled “A Visit to the Renowned Sushi Dai Honkan in Tsukiji Market” on its Toyosu Sushi Dai page. Both restaurants are good. They are not the same restaurant.

“You can eat at Sushi Dai in the Tsukiji fish market”

Moved October 2018. Sushi Dai relocated with the wholesale market to Toyosu; its own listing says so from its old Tsukiji 5-2-1 address. No branch stayed behind.

“Sushi Dai Honkan is Sushi Dai’s Tsukiji branch”

Different business entirely. Separate management, different hours (10:30 vs dawn), different payment (cards vs cash), different seating (40 over three floors vs one counter). The collision, not a branch — see the departure board.

“Sushi Dai’s omakase is ¥4,500”

¥5,500 as of 2026, verified by an itemised diner receipt and the listing’s ¥5,000–5,999 band. It was ¥5,000 in 2023; ¥4,500 is pre-2023 and still everywhere. Price ladder.

“Daiwa Sushi is right next to Sushi Dai”

Different block, different building. Daiwa is in Block 5 — the fruit and vegetable building, 1F — a several-minute walk from Sushi Dai’s Block 6. The co-operative publishes turn-by-turn directions precisely because everyone gets this wrong. Chapter 03.

The Toyosu slot queue, mechanically

The single genuine queue in either market — and it is not a queue for a table, it is a queue for a slot. The sources disagree on “opening time” (the co-operative says 5:30, the listing says 6:00) because they describe different steps of one sequence:

  1. 04:00Iwata and Senriken cafés open in Block 6 — coffee, toast, omurice, cream stew. Cash only. This is where you wait, not in the line.
  2. ~04:45Be in the line on the 3F bridge/deck outside the market entrance if you want the first seating. A 4:45 arrival has found 20–30 people already ahead.
  3. 05:00The 3F floor opens; security walks the line in to a desk outside the still-closed shop. Staff take your name, party size, preferred time and your order. You get a slip with a guide time — then you leave and come back.
  4. ~05:30Shop doors open behind the desk. The slot gap is the classic moment for the free tuna-auction walkway — the auction peaks 05:30–06:30, one floor away.
  5. 06:00First seating: about 15–18 diners at the counter, one chef per roughly four guests, the omakase runs about an hour — and nobody rushes you afterwards.
  6. 07:30Second seating. After this, guide times roll against seat availability and party size — there is no published fixed grid; observed slips say 7:40, 8:40, 11:40. Treat “about six sittings” claims as shape, not schedule.
  7. 14:00Close. Reception ends earlier once slots run out — a cancellation-waiting board goes out front, and re-reception happens only on no-shows.

Useful fine print: one person can hold the slot — a first-hand account confirms only one of the group must be present at the desk — but keep the rest of the party within a minute’s walk, because staff won’t wait if you’re absent at your turn. And avoid the word “reservation” entirely: no advance booking of any kind exists — no phone, no web, no hotel concierge, no operator. Same-day desk only.

Daiwa Sushi: the easier door most people can’t find

Daiwa Sushi 大和寿司 sits in Block 5, the fruit and vegetable building, 1F — not beside Sushi Dai, and that’s exactly why its queue is shorter: most visitors never look in the produce building. From the Yurikamome-connected 3F deck, take the left-hand passage, pass the produce-market gate, turn left at the first fork, and take the lift or stairs at the first corner down to 1F — the operator’s own written directions.

New for 2026, and its own co-operative page hasn’t caught up: Daiwa now takes online bookings through TableCheck — parties of one or two only, weekdays 07:00–10:00 and Saturdays 08:00–09:30, small booking fee. Outside those windows it’s walk-in, and the chef’s own advice is that 08:00–09:30 and post-10:00 are easy, with Tuesdays and Thursdays quietest. The co-operative’s directory still says no reservations and cash only — while 2026 diners report cards and e-money working. Carry cash anyway: that failure mode is trivial, the other one isn’t.

The honest take: a widely-read Toyosu guide writer puts it bluntly — given how many good counters Toyosu holds, the Sushi Dai queue is not worth the hassle. Daiwa, Sushi Bun and a dozen other in-market counters serve the same market’s fish with little or no wait. The queue measures fame, not quality. Do it as a pilgrimage if jet lag hands you 4 a.m. for free; don’t set an alarm for it believing there’s no alternative.

If you want the whole dawn choreographed — and the storefront-slot ritual is precisely the part that intimidates — the Toyosu tuna auction morning with a Sushi Dai or Bentomi breakfast (★ 4.6 · 164 reviews · ~$114, 31 October 2026) bundles the auction walkway, the market walk and the breakfast into one guided dawn. Be clear-eyed about what you’re buying: no operator has a priority channel — the desk works down one physical line for everyone — so the value is the solved 4 a.m. logistics and a guide running the ritual with you, plus a no-queue fallback counter when slots are gone. Right for first-timers who want one shot at the dream morning; wrong if you enjoy figuring it out solo. More formats on the auction tours page.

The dawn truth at Tsukiji: kaisendon, not nigiri

Here is the fact that breaks most itineraries: the Tsukiji outer market opens early, but its sushi restaurants largely don’t. Of the 24 sushi shops on the official association list, the great majority open at 10:00 or 11:00 — Sushisei at 10:00, Sushikuni at 10:00, Tsukijiya at 10:00, the Honkan at 10:30, Hidetoku at 11:00. Counter nigiri at dawn became a Toyosu proposition in 2018. What Tsukiji does at dawn — and does well — is the seafood bowl, plus two chains that never close at all.

Two footnotes with honest flags. Sushizanmai Bekkan also runs 24 hours; Tsukiji Sushi Ichiban advertises 24 hours on its own marketing site rather than the association listing — verify before staking a 3 a.m. plan on it. And the 14:00–15:30 window when some counters mark down fatty tuna is a single dated observation from 2026, not a rule — treat it as a pleasant surprise, never a plan.

The Sunday/Wednesday trap: Toyosu closes on all Sundays and most Wednesdays (45 of 52 in 2026) — Sushi Dai, Daiwa and Sushi Bun all shut. At Tsukiji roughly two Wednesdays a month are closure days, and on Sundays only about a third of shops open while tourist crowds stay full-strength — the worst ratio of the week. On those days your dawn options are exactly two: the 24-hour shops and Kanno. Check the calendars before setting an alarm.

The reverse itinerary works beautifully on trading days: Toyosu counters for the 06:00–08:00 window, then back west for Tsukiji grazing at 09:00–11:00 — 35–45 minutes door to door, and the getting-there page solves the pre-5:45 train problem (first Yurikamome from Toyosu station 05:15; the Shimbashi Yurikamome’s 05:45 start is the classic mistake).

The departure board: who moved, who stayed

The 2018 split was clean in law and muddled in memory: the metropolitan inner market and its licensed tenants moved to Toyosu on 11 October 2018; the privately-owned outer market never moved because it was never part of the wholesale market at all. The consolation in the split: outer-market vendors buy at Toyosu every morning and truck the fish back, so at Tsukiji you still eat Toyosu-grade fish — just not at a dawn nigiri counter. Full market-vs-market context on the history page.

Counter rules the guidebooks skip

  • One full portion per person. Sushi Bun states it in writing and the co-operative repeats it market-wide: you may not split one set between two people. It’s seat economics at an 18-seat counter, not stinginess — and it’s the rule visitors break most.
  • Order before you sit — sometimes before you queue. Sushi Dai’s desk takes your order at 05:00, hours before you eat; Sushi Bun takes it in the line before seating. Reading a menu once seated is not how these counters work.
  • Full-turnover seating. When busy, Sushi Bun seats a new group only after every current diner has left. Don’t expect to slide into a single free stool.
  • Finish, then linger or leave — both are true. The co-operative asks diners to leave promptly; Sushi Dai diners consistently report zero pressure and happily added pieces at the end. Order more or go; just don’t camp.
  • Photography: ask first, every time. Sushi Bun’s page carries a notice declining photography inside the shop — a notice flagged by visitors as possibly predating the 2018 move, but treat it as live until the counter says otherwise. Tsukiji’s official manners list says the same market-wide: some shops post bans, usually in Japanese only.
  • No eating while walking — both markets, and at Toyosu the ban explicitly includes the walkway benches. Eat at the counter, or standing in front of the Tsukiji shop that sold it. More on the visiting responsibly page.
  • Before 9:00, Tsukiji’s lanes belong to the buyers. A 07:00 kaisendon makes you a guest in a workplace: stay out of trolley lanes, step aside for photos.
  • Cash, drawn in advance. Sushi Dai, Sushi Bun, Iwata and Senriken are cash only — Sushi Dai’s listing refuses credit, e-money and QR alike. There is no reliable visitor ATM inside Toyosu Market; the Seven Bank machine at Plat Tsukiji takes foreign cards. Carry ¥10,000–15,000 per person. Full details on the practical page.
  • Luggage rules bite at dawn. Suitcases and strollers are restricted inside Toyosu, which has no lockers at all — they’re at Shijō-mae Station. At Tsukiji, Plat Tsukiji stores strollers for ¥500.

One group-size note with a date on it: on 1 December 2025 the Tsukiji association declared overtourism, asked visitors to avoid the year-end crush, and asked operators to refrain from guided food strolls in the district — a December-targeted request that had not been reissued as of 31 October 2026. Self-guided grazing is what the association asked for; if you want a guided format, food tours and classes that sit off the lanes travel lighter than a big group in Nishi-dōri.

And if the queue culture repels you entirely — families especially, for whom a 4:45 a.m. start, an 18-seat counter, no high chairs and a one-portion rule stack up badly — flip the format: at a sushi-making class the market fish still stars, but you hold the knife. The Tsukiji sushi-making class with a pro chef (★ 4.9 · 858 reviews · ~$50) is the best-reviewed of the lot — a civilised hour, no line, and children are an asset rather than a liability. It costs less than the Sushi Dai set plus the taxi that gets you there.
