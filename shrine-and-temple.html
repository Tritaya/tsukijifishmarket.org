Shrine & temple · Tsukiji 築地 · verified 2 August 2026

The guardian shrine and the temple that looks like India

At opposite ends of the outer market stand its two free counterpoints: Namiyoke Inari 波除稲荷神社, a tiny 1659 shrine where the sushi, prawn, egg, monkfish, live-fish and kelp trades raised stone memorials to the creatures they sell, and Tsukiji Hongan-ji 築地本願寺, a 1934 Buddhist temple in ancient-Indian dress with a brand-new Italian pipe organ. Both run their own calendars — which means both are open on exactly the days the lanes and Toyosu are shut.

TL;DR

  • Both are free, and both ignore the market's calendar. Hongan-ji's main hall opens 6:00–16:00 every day of the year; Namiyoke publishes no closed days at all. On the Sundays, holidays and Wednesdays that empty half of Tsukiji, these two are open.
  • Namiyoke's real treasure isn't the giant lions — it's the row of stone mounds raised by named trade associations to sushi, prawns, monkfish, live fish, eggs and kelp, each still receiving an annual memorial rite. Nothing else like it exists in Tokyo.
  • Hongan-ji sells no goshuin, no amulets, no fortunes — by doctrine, not oversight. What it offers instead: a 7:00 morning service, free lunchtime organ concerts, and an 18-dish breakfast you book 30 days out at midnight — mechanics in chapter 06.
  • They're 600–700 m apart — 8–10 minutes on foot, and the walk between them is the outer market. Start at the temple (the Hibiya Line's Tsukiji Exit 1 surfaces inside its grounds), finish at the shrine at the dead end of the lanes.

Four myths this page exists to kill

“The giant lion heads are the surviving 1848 originals”

Newly carved in 1990 and 2002. Chuo City's own cultural-property record is explicit: the registered 1848 pair — the only heads to escape the fires of the 1923 earthquake — are small, gilded, and kept inside the main hall flanking the deity. The courtyard giants are magnificent modern reconstructions. Full story in chapter 03.

“The Shishi Matsuri only happens every three years”

It's every June. The grand rite falls on 10 June every year; only the full hon-matsuri format — the thousand-can mikoshi plus a lion — is triennial. Even the 2026 off-year carried two. Details in chapter 04.

“Collect a goshuin at Tsukiji Hongan-ji”

The temple offers none — by doctrine. Its own visitor page states it prepares no omikuji, no goshuin and no omamori, in accordance with Jodo Shinshu teaching. The goshuin desk you want is Namiyoke's, 8 minutes away, ¥500, 9:00–17:00.

“Hongan-ji is closed for restoration until the end of 2026”

Open 6:00–16:00 throughout. The conservation works (19 Aug 2024 → 25 Dec 2026) bring weekday noise and diverted walkways, nothing more — and the contractors are off on weekends and holidays, so Saturday and Sunday visits are quiet.

Two of those myths appear on official pages — this district's paperwork ages badly. The full list of stale-source traps is in chapter 07.

Namiyoke: the shrine the market prayed to

“Tsukiji” means built land, and Namiyoke stands on the last, storm-battered phase of the 1658 landfill that created it — the shrine's name means wave-warding, and its strapline still reads “ward off calamity, ride out the waves.” It has occupied the same spot since 1659, nearly three centuries before the wholesale market opened next door in 1935 and now eight years after that market left for Toyosu. It was the market's guardian by devotion, not tenancy — which is why the devotion continues.

The proof is the thing no sixty-second tour stop explains: the row of stone mounds 塚, memorials raised by named trade associations to the creatures they sell, each with its own annual rite still performed by the association that built it.

Sushi-zuka すし塚 · 1 Nov 1972

The sushi mound

Erected by the Tokyo sushi traders' association, the inscription brushed by then-Deputy Prime Minister Miki Takeo. Every 1 November — National Sushi Day — sushi-topping fish are laid on the mound in a memorial rite for the fish that became your breakfast.

Ebi-zuka 海老塚 · Sep 1973

The prawn mound

Raised by the Toten-kai tempura cooperative and Ebi-no-Daimaru; each July, prawns and tempura vegetables are offered. The tempura you'll eat in the lanes has its own memorial.

Anko-zuka 鮟鱇塚 · Jul 1973

The monkfish mound

Raised by a single wholesaler, Miura Hiroo, continuing his late father's wish — the rite comes each late October–November, as monkfish season opens.

Kaigyo-zuka 活魚塚 · May 1984

The live-fish mound

By the live-fish wholesalers' union — a trade now at Toyosu. At the May–June rite a superbly ikejime-dressed sea bream is offered at the mound, then the main hall, before 100-plus members.

Tamago-zuka 玉子塚 · Nov 1993

The egg mound

By the Tokyo egg-processors' association on its 30th anniversary — for the eggs consumed making the thick tamagoyaki the sushi counters buy. Rite each October.

Kombu-zuka 昆布塚 · 8 Dec 2016

The kelp mound

The newest, inscribed with hirome — the old word for kombu first recorded in 797. Kelp underwrites the dashi in nearly everything you'll taste here.

Alongside them: a clam stone donated by a wholesaler, two fish-quay stones of 1925 and 1929 recording the trade names of the brokers who moved here after the earthquake, and a Yoshinoya monument (2016) — the gyudon chain followed the fish market from Nihonbashi to Tsukiji in 1923 and opened its “Tsukiji No. 1 store” in 1959. Budget 25–35 minutes to read it all; 15 if you only look.

The lion heads, precisely

The two giants get the photographs: the 1-tonne male Yakuyoke Tenjo O-Jishi in its own pavilion — 2.4 m high, 3.3 m wide, carved from a single kurobe trunk by Chida Seiun of Ishikawa for the shrine's 330th anniversary in 1990 — and the vermilion, black-toothed female Ohaguro Jishi of 2002 (~700 kg) in the Benzaiten hall. The ward-registered cultural property, though, is the pair you can miss entirely: the small 1848 heads inside the main hall, gilded, vermilion-lacquered inside lips and nostrils, fitted with red-yak hair in a 1927 repair — the only heads to escape the fires of the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake, registered by Chuo ward on 1 April 1992. New glass doors carved with a dragon and tiger were fitted to the hall front precisely so the interior can be seen when the office is closed — which is also the shrine's only published hint about hours: the precinct has no published gate times and no published closed days, and the only window the shrine itself stands behind is the staffed office, 9:00–17:00.

  • Goshuin: main shrine ¥500, Benzaiten ¥500, paper-cut version ¥1,500, goshuincho ¥2,000 — desk 9:00–17:00, cash. Seven Lucky Gods stamps are issued on days containing a 7 (the 7th, 17th and 27th).
  • Touch nothing except the snake stone 蛇石 — the one object you are invited to stroke, for money luck. Wish-sticks placed on the great lion's tongue are burned once a year at the December Ohitaki rite; leave them be.
  • Also 1838: the pair of cast-iron rainwater basins (ward-registered 1993), donated by the stevedores who unloaded the Owari domain's cargo here — the shrine predates the fish market by centuries, and the basins prove it.
  • Festival-limited goshuin are pre-written sheets only — issued as ready-made kakioki, never brushed into your book, and quantity-limited.
  • No toilets, no lockers, no car park. The nearest of all three is Plat Tsukiji, the outer market's information centre a few minutes north (stroller storage ¥500).

The Shishi Matsuri: every June, big every third year

The rule, from the shrine's own annual-events page: the grand rite is on 10 June every year, with the associated festival days on the nearest Friday-to-Sunday. What rotates is the format. In an ordinary year (kage-matsuri), one of the three great objects — the thousand-can mikoshi, the male lion, the female lion — is carried on the Sunday. In the once-in-three-years hon-matsuri, the mikoshi goes out plus a lion. 2025 was a confirmed hon-matsuri that exceeded even that: all three carried together on 15 June, marking a double centenary. 2026 (10–14 June) was an off-year that still carried two, opening the shrine's 370th-anniversary programme.

When is the next hon-matsuri? The sources disagree, so we won't pretend to know. The Chuo City tourism portal states the next hon-matsuri is 2027; the shrine's own three-year rule counted from 2025, and a Chuo City broadcast from August 2025 (“next in three years”), both point to 2028. The three-year rhythm demonstrably slipped around the pandemic. If your trip hinges on it, check namiyoke.or.jp or call the shrine (03-3541-8451, 9:00–17:00) in April or May — that is what we'll be doing.

Smaller calendar marks worth knowing: the 7th of each month unveils one of the Seven Lucky Gods on a monthly rotation (the matching goshuin is issued on the 7th, 17th and 27th); the rooster days of November bring the Tsukiji Tori-no-ichi rake market (¥1,000 kakkome talismans, with a lottery for a gold-leaf charm); and the two weeping ginkgos are at their best late November to early December.

Hongan-ji: the temple that looks like India

First, what it is not: a Hindu temple, or an Indian one. Tsukiji Hongan-ji is a temple of Jodo Shinshu Buddhism (Hongwanji school — the -w- spelling is the temple's own romanisation), on ground reclaimed after the great fire of 1657, the very landfill project whose final phase produced Namiyoke's site a year later. The ancient-Indian exterior — the dome, the stone, the winged lion over the main entrance — is the 1934 design of Ito Chuta, honorary professor at Tokyo Imperial University, born of his Asian architectural travels. In 2014 the main hall, stone wall and gate pillars were designated national Important Cultural Property. Inside, it is conventionally Jodo Shinshu: chairs, not tatami; the worshippers' space larger than the clergy's; the greeting a quiet nembutsu, never a clap — clapping belongs at the shrine up the road.

The building rewards a slow circuit: stained glass, chandeliers, lotus-and-animal carvings on the stair balustrades, and Ito's thirteen hidden animal sculptures — oxen, lions, horses, elephants, peacocks, monkeys and assorted mythical beasts — scattered through the halls. The temple's own beginner page says the reason each animal sits where it does “remains a mystery”, which makes it an officially endorsed scavenger hunt, and the best free entertainment for children in the whole district. Every main-hall visitor can pick up a free monthly commemorative worship card.

  • Hours: main hall 6:00–16:00, every day of the year, closing after the evening service — it opens at the same hour the market lanes do, so the 7:00 morning service slots neatly after an early first bite. Daily services 7:00, 9:30 and 14:00.
  • Getting in: Hibiya Line “Tsukiji” Exit 1 is directly connected — you surface inside the temple grounds. Full routings on getting there.
  • The works: conservation of the Important Cultural Property runs 19 Aug 2024 – 25 Dec 2026 — roof re-coppering, waterproofing, seismic strengthening, plus a new barrier-free lift in the south courtyard. Hall open throughout; weekday noise; contractors off weekends and holidays; reception on the 1st floor since April 2026.
  • Photography: a written regulation has been in force since 1 May 2022. Personal photos and social posts: fine. Live streaming, commercial or promotional shooting, and photographing private memorial services without the family's permission: restricted, and staff may require deletion.
  • Facilities: toilets and accessible toilets in all three buildings, a nursing room at the Information Center, lift access to the hall, a published barrier-free map — the district's best free rest stop, and its best bad-weather shelter (the hall is indoors and seated; the vast stone forecourt, mind, is shadeless in August).
  • Eating & buying beyond the cafe: Shisui, the ground-floor restaurant, does shojin ryori and the temple's signature monzen soba; the Official Shop sells tenugui and totes using the hall's own motifs; and the souvenir counter carries two things you can't get elsewhere — temple senbei made by Ginza's Matsuzaki from rice first offered to the Buddha, and manju by Shiose Sohonke, purveyor to the Imperial Household.

The pipe organ — and the “last Friday” trap

The organ you hear today is not the famous 1970 instrument. That organ, donated by the Bukkyo Dendo Kyokai, was given a farewell concert in January 2025 and dismantled the following month; its successor — built in Italy, 2,566 pipes from 3 cm to over a metre, shipped by sea in crates and assembled by Italian craftsmen working overnight — was dedicated in November 2025, the gift of the same foundation. The temple describes the organ front as six “mountains”, one for each character of Namu Amida Butsu.

You can hear it free at the lunchtime concerts (running monthly since January 2006, about 500 attend, no booking — be seated before the 12:10 dharma talk; music from 12:20; each concert is also streamed free on the temple's YouTube channel with a three-day catch-up window).

“The organ concert is on the last Friday of every month”

Even the temple's own introduction page says this — and its own schedule contradicts it. In 2026, 19 June was the third Friday and 24 July the fourth. Always read the published schedule, never the rule.

The 18-dish breakfast, mechanically

Cafe Tsumugi, inside the temple's Information Center, serves the district's most civilised morning: the 18-dish breakfast — sixteen small dishes plus rice porridge and miso soup, eighteen items in all, named for the 18th of Amida's 48 vows, the one that promises never to abandon you. The cheaper temple breakfast (¥1,760) is built on named outer-market suppliers: salt-koji-grilled silver salmon from Uogashi Kitada, tamagoyaki from Tsukiji Shoro, kombu tsukudani from Edoichi Iida. Breakfast runs 8:00–10:30 only, the cafe itself 8:00–18:00, year-round.

“The 18-dish breakfast is 2,200 yen — just turn up”

¥2,640 including tax since 5 January 2026 (the temple's own price-revision notice), and the popular dish routinely sells out before the 10:30 cut-off — only a reservation guarantees a portion.

Booking it (Toreta, the cafe's own system)

  • Reservations open 30 days ahead — counting the booking day — at 00:00. The page's own worked example: for 22 February, booking opens at midnight on 24 January. Set an alarm; this is the whole game.
  • Four timed seatings start between 8:00 and 10:00, each capped at 90 minutes; arrive about five minutes early.
  • A seat reservation guarantees your 18-dish portion — the stated policy, and the entire reason to book rather than queue.
  • No same-day bookings. Walk-ins join a door queue seated in the gaps between booked cohorts — so people wait even when tables look free.
  • Pay on leaving, never in advance — the booking page warns explicitly that any site asking for prepayment is fake. Cards, PayPay and e-money accepted.
  • Groups of five or more may be split across tables; more than 15 minutes late without contact and your table may go; phone booking via an AI line on 03-5565-5581. No vegan and no halal options — stated plainly by the cafe.

How early is early enough without a booking? The temple publishes no capacity figures — what follows is diner-reported, not official: roughly 120 portions a day, about 40 people already queueing at 9:00, the 18-dish set gone by around 9:50; one weekday walk-in at 7:30 got in without trouble. Treat those numbers as anecdotes pointing one direction: book, or arrive before 8:00 on a weekday. Miss both and the cheaper temple breakfast is usually still available — or the lanes are right there, with an entirely different kind of breakfast.

Prices and booking terms read from the temple's and cafe's own pages, 2 August 2026 — menus drift; the Toreta page is the truth.

The quiet plan: a morning that costs nothing

These two are the district's pressure-release valves — the shrine near-empty before 8:30 and calm again after 14:30, the temple's forecourt spacious at any hour, and both at their best exactly when the lanes are at their worst. (Context worth knowing: in December 2025 the outer market's own trade council declared overtourism and asked operators to pause guided food tours in the lanes for that month. The request was December-scoped and had not been reissued as of 31 October 2026 — but the congestion it answered is real, and these two sites are the antidote.)

  1. 06:00Hongan-ji's main hall opens — the same hour the lanes wake. Empty forecourt, best light on the east-facing facade.
  2. 07:00Morning service in the main hall. Sit at the back; a solo visitor can simply walk in where a tour group cannot.
  3. 08:00Breakfast seating at Cafe Tsumugi — if you booked 30 days ago. If not, you're first in the walk-in queue.
  4. 09:30Walk the lanes south-east — the 8–10 minutes between temple and shrine is the outer market. Several market walks run this same line with a guide decoding the stalls.
  5. 10:30Namiyoke: the mounds, both lions, the snake stone; goshuin desk open since 9:00. Done by 11:15, ahead of the midday crush.
  6. 12:10On concert Fridays: back at the temple, seated for the dharma talk; organ from 12:20. Total spend so far: ¥0.
  7. 16:00The hall closes after the evening service — the shrine precinct, with no published closing time, is your last stop standing.

With children, this pair beats the lanes

Namiyoke reads to a child as a treasure hunt — two lion heads the size of a car, a snake stone you're allowed to rub, a fox someone anonymously re-dresses in a red bib, a full zodiac of twelve animals, and mounds for sushi, prawns and eggs. The temple is the genuinely easy one: a traffic-free forecourt with room to run, chair seating, toilets and a nursing room in every building, the free monthly worship card as a collectable, and Ito Chuta's thirteen hidden animals as an officially endorsed scavenger hunt (realistic from about age 4). The pinch point is the lane approach to the shrine, not the shrine — hold hands, and the official advice is to leave strollers at Plat Tsukiji (¥500) rather than push them through. An 8:00 cafe breakfast is a far more civilised opening move with a toddler than queueing for raw fish.

Stale-source watch: when even the official pages are wrong

This pair attracts official misinformation the way the auction attracts 2018 blog posts. What we found, so you don't trust it:

  • Chuo City's own cultural-property pages (posted 2023, reprinting 2013 columns) still place the shrine at the entrance to the operating Tsukiji wholesale market — which moved to Toyosu in October 2018. The scholarship is excellent; the geography is seven years stale.
  • The Chuo City Tourism Association's member listing for Namiyoke gives the address and phone number of Teppozu Inari — a different shrine 1.5 km away. Namiyoke is at 6-20-37 Tsukiji, 03-3541-8451.
  • The same association's portal says the next hon-matsuri is 2027; a Chuo City broadcast and the shrine's own rule point to 2028. See chapter 04 — call the shrine in spring.
  • The temple's own beginner page still says the organ concert is “the last Friday of every month”; its own 2026 schedule broke that rule twice.
  • Aggregators still quote ¥2,200 for the 18-dish breakfast (pre-January 2026) and describe the old 1970 organ with “about 2,000 pipes”.
  • Any itinerary pairing the shrine with Sushi Dai or Daiwa is pre-2018 — both counters are at Toyosu now.

Do you need a tour for any of this? Honestly: no — both sites are free, unticketed and five minutes from a subway exit, and that's the point of them. The genuine cases for a guide: every explanatory panel at Namiyoke is Japanese-only, so a good private guide can decode the mounds on the spot; and architecture specialists earn their fee at Hongan-ji, where the Ito Chuta reading rewards expertise. Most market walking tours give the shrine sixty seconds as their turnaround point — if you take one for the food, walk back afterwards and read the mounds properly yourself.
