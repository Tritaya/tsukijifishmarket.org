Beyond eating: the market teaches. Almost none of it is in English.

Tsukiji's third axis after shop and eat is learn — and the 2026 map is inverted from what English guides show. The sushi classes everyone books (¥15,400–17,600 direct, ~$50–175 on marketplaces) sit alongside an almost entirely unreported layer taught by the shopkeepers themselves for ¥0–4,500: fish filleting in the market's own kitchen, knife sharpening taught by the president of Aritsugu, a six-grade katsuobushi tasting, a free shaving lesson at a wholesaler's counter. This page maps both layers honestly — including which one you can actually get into.

TL;DR

  • Two supplies, never one. Marketplaces list ten-plus "Tsukiji classes"; the market's own upcoming list held exactly two items on 2 August 2026. The double-digit counts are commercial products; the market's own programme is small, cheap, Japanese and oversubscribed.
  • The cheapest instruction is the best — and the hardest to reach. Shopkeeper-taught seminars run ¥2,000–4,500 with 4–16 seats, announced ~3 weeks out on tsukiji.or.jp (named as plain text on purpose — the English site links none of it), booked first-come on Peatix. Essentially everything recent sold out.
  • No Japanese, fixed dates? Book an English sushi class and don't feel short-changed: guaranteed seat, free cancellation, and it adds nobody to lanes the market says are over capacity. The honest comparison is in chapter 05.
  • Rainy day? This is the category. The lanes have no roof and no arcade; a booked indoor class is the district's only genuinely weather-proof plan.

Three myths this page exists to kill

“You can book a cooking class at the Tsukiji Uogashi Studio”

It's a room for hire, not a school. The studio rents at ¥66,000–176,000 per booking, enquiries by email only. But the best classes in Tsukiji happen inside it — the council's own seminars and the free festival classes. Book the class, never the room. More in chapter 03 and on the Uogashi building page.

“There's a tuna-cutting show at Tsukiji fish market”

No market-run show exists. The reliable weekly cuts are inside restaurants — one at Tsukiji, one at Toyosu, both Saturdays from 11:30. The occasional free demonstrations at market events are announced days ahead, if at all. Full mechanics in chapter 07.

“The classes are all bookable in English”

Precisely backwards. The commercial classes are in English; almost nothing else is. Tamazushi asks foreign guests to bring an interpreter; Fushitaka books by phone in Japanese; every council seminar page is Japanese. The one bridge: Peatix's checkout runs in English and takes foreign cards.

The Tsukiwaza discovery: the market's own curriculum

The single best finding in our research: the market council runs a named, structured, multi-year seminar programme — Tsukiwaza 築技セミナー — taught by the shopkeepers themselves in the market's own kitchen studio. Thirteen editions of the beginners' filleting course alone. Its stated purpose is food-town-building that makes you better at cooking, not tourism — and the content shows it. Almost none of this appears in English anywhere, including on the market's own English site, which says you can "learn how to cut fish" and then links nothing.

Read the gate: three of the best sessions are alumni-only — open exclusively to people who have done the council's own "first fish filleting" course (the free festival classes explicitly do not count). The ¥4,000 entry seminar is the door to everything else: two hours, aji filleted until it clicks, and you take home the fish you cut. That progression — entry course → flounder, refresher, sharpening with Aritsugu's president — is the single most actionable finding here for a Tokyo resident or repeat visitor, and nobody explains it in English.

How booking actually works (nobody has written this down in English)

There is no timetable. Sessions are announced individually on the council's event index — tsukiji.or.jp/event, plain text on purpose — each with its own application opening moment: the 23 August 2026 flounder class opened at 12:00 on 3 August, exactly 20 days ahead. Booking runs on per-event Peatix pages (tsukijiYYYYMMDD.peatix.com). The listing is Japanese-only, but Peatix's checkout has a full English interface and takes international cards — the one bridge for a non-Japanese speaker. First-come, no published waitlist; of the recent sessions on the council's own index, essentially every one carried a "capacity reached" banner. Treat "apply the hour it opens" as the working assumption.

Two footnotes: the upcoming list is thin by design — on 2 August 2026 it held two items against 45 in the archive, clustering into the three festival weeks and early December. And anything in the Uogashi Studio inherits that building's stricter calendar (closed every Sunday and public holiday) — the 8 July abalone seminar was relocated for exactly that reason. Check the calendars before you plan around a session. Groups of 20–50 have a separate official answer: the ¥500-a-head Maruwakari classroom course, booked by fax, whose own leaflet states it is not an outer-market tour.

The sharpening class, disambiguated (both halves are true)

The knife-sharpening story needs both sentences published together. The standing, bookable-any-month class is at Toyosu, not Tsukiji: Aritsugu's own one-to-one at its Toyosu Market store — ¥2,200 including tax paid on the day, one group of up to two people, 12:00 start, held irregularly. Book through the calendar on its own site more than seven days ahead or by phone within seven; the booking exists only when the confirmation email arrives; changes at least three days out; trade competitors excluded, and video filming is banned — the only explicit filming ban we found attached to any class in this district. Whether it is taught in English is stated nowhere, so we won't claim it. Tsukiji, meanwhile, gets the occasional seminar above — five seats, alumni-gated, taught by the same house's president in person. And if you just want an edge while you're at Tsukiji: Aritsugu's Tsukiji shop offers informal instruction on request, free — context on the knives & shopping page.

The bookable-in-English layer, honestly ranked

If your dates are fixed and your Japanese is not, the commercial class is not a consolation prize — it is the only one you can actually get into, and what your money buys is real: English instruction, a guaranteed seat on a fixed date, free cancellation (mostly to 24 hours out), and dietary accommodation you can declare in advance. It is also the format the market's own December 2025 overtourism request implicitly points towards — a class puts you indoors and adds nobody to the lanes. The full catalogue lives at sushi classes; the three we'd actually book:

  • Tsukiji sushi-making class with a pro chef (★ 4.9 · 758 reviews · ~$53) — the flagship of the whole category and the volume pick: the highest-reviewed class near the market at the price of a mid-tier lunch. Best for anyone who has already eaten the market and wants the skill, not another walk.
  • Tsukiji market walk + sushi cooking class (★ 4.7 · 126 reviews · ~$95) — the walk-then-cook shape: lanes first, class after, which is exactly the sequencing that works (shops wind down after noon; the class doesn't). Best for first-timers who haven't walked Tsukiji yet.
  • Sushi making with a pro chef + Tsukiji market tour (★ 4.8 · 91 reviews · ~$94) — the longer pro-chef variant of the same combo, at four hours the fullest single morning in this category. Best if the class is the centrepiece of your day rather than an add-on.
  • The direct-booking benchmark, for calibration: Tsukiji Cooking (named as plain text — book on its own nine-language site) publishes dated slots at ¥15,400 class-only, 11:00–13:00, or ¥17,600 with a market walk, 10:00–13:00. The class-only slot never enters the lanes at all. If a marketplace product costs materially more than that, the difference should be buying you something you can name.

And the honest asterisk on the whole section: the market's own two-hour filleting seminar costs ¥4,000 and is a better product — a shopkeeper of thirty years, a fish you take home. The difference is not quality. It is language and access, which is exactly what chapter 03 is for.

Tamazushi: the century-old house that teaches at its own counter

Almost invisible in English coverage: Tsukiji Tamazushi, an Edomae sushi house of a century's standing, runs a proper counter-side class — you put on the chef's white coat and hat, stand at the real counter, and leave with a certificate and an ukiyo-e coaster. Named as plain text deliberately: booking is by web form on its own site, in Japanese only — the house asks foreign guests to bring an interpreter guide.

  • Standard — ¥11,000 incl. tax (Harumi-dōri branch or Ginza): six nigiri, a hand-roll tasting, a comparison against the chef's own — and this is where two famous questions actually live: grating and tasting real wasabi, and a proper sushi-restaurant tamagoyaki show.
  • Premium — ¥16,500: adds watching a professional break down a large fish and a shari-kiri rice-seasoning exercise.
  • Luxury — ¥27,500, minimum two people, at the group's flagship venues: wasabi on a traditional sharkskin grater, live searing, a four-knife demonstration; an optional 90-minute course-meal version runs ¥38,500.

The teeth in the small print: not run on Sundays or public holidays; vegetarians and allergies must be declared in advance; party-size changes only to the day before; cancellation 30% a week out, 50% the day before, 100% on the day. For a family without Japanese, the honest answer remains an English sushi class — this one is for visitors who travel with an interpreter or a Japanese-speaking friend, and for them it is the real thing.

The tuna-cutting shows, corrected

What exists is not what listings imply. There is no market-run, scheduled tuna-cutting show for visitors inside Tsukiji Outer Market. The reliable weekly cuts are restaurant events — you buy lunch, not a ticket — and the big wholesalers who advertise kaitai shows sell them as catering for weddings and corporate parties, not to walk-up visitors.

A conflict we're flagging rather than hiding: an undated LIVE JAPAN feature says Sushi Ichiban's show runs Saturdays and Sundays from 10:00, alternating between two branches — the restaurant's own 2026 posts say every Saturday from 11:30 at 4-chōme. Act on the shop's own current statement, and phone before travelling for this. At a 39-seat restaurant, arriving at 11:25 is not a plan: enter early and order before the cut starts, because the rare cuts can sell out during the show.

And keep the categories straight: a kaitai show is a restaurant's lunch theatre. The tuna auction is a wholesale transaction at Toyosu at dawn, watched from a free walkway or a lottery deck. Neither has anything to do with the other.

The free layer: taught by the shopkeeper, priced at zero

The free stuff is not a gimmick — it is the best stuff, and English guides miss it entirely. Three formats:

The 09:00 classroom with the map nobody else gets

The council's "knack of shopping at Tsukiji" classroom — how to shop the specialist lanes when the shops feel intimidating — is the market's only genuinely drop-in session: free, no booking at all, 09:00–09:30 (doors 08:45) in the Shokumachi Studio next to the information centre. Attendees get a Tsukiji shopping map handed out to nobody else. It ran on 25/26 November and 2 December 2025; if the pattern holds, watch the event index in late November 2026.

Fushitaka's shaving lesson — and why its web page cannot be trusted

Phone. Always phone. Fushitaka, a katsuobushi wholesaler, teaches a free lesson at its counter — shaving a rock-hard bonito block into feathery flakes, and setting the plane blade of your own kezuriki if you bring one, which is the part that actually determines whether you get shavings or powder. One group of up to two people per trading day, 13:00, booked by telephone only (03-3541-0918, calls 06:30–14:00, Japanese; email expressly refused). It is suspended 1 December–5 January and 1 August–10 September — but read at source on 2 August 2026, inside that summer blackout, the live page showed no suspension notice at all: the notices sit commented out in the page's HTML and are toggled by hand. The page cannot be trusted about whether the class is running. The phone call can.

The festival classes — the best value in Tokyo food tourism

Three times a year — the winter festival in late January, spring in late April/early May, autumn in late October/early November — the shopping-street association runs free, advance-booked food-education classes in the Uogashi Studio, taught by the presidents and directors of the market's own houses. The verified 2026 line-ups: fish filleting and a kombu-dashi cup-by-cup comparison in January; grater-by-grater tasting with shellfish shucking, a four-dashi katsuobushi comparison, and — the single best thing for a child in Tsukiji — hands-on tamagoyaki rolling taught by Tsukiji Shouro つきぢ松露, in ten-minute slots of six people, in the spring. All free. All filled. The market's paid kids' sessions (hand-opening a sardine, "become a kombu doctor" with a 130-year-old kombu house — ¥2,000 per child-plus-guardian) filled weeks ahead too.

One caution we owe you: Shouro's own promotional copy has described an open free class — but its official site publishes no class page, and the only documented sessions are the advance-booked festival slots. Do not plan a trip around walking in. Ask at the shop.

The correction that keeps coming back: the Uogashi Studio

Every one of these sessions happens in the Uogashi Studio, the teaching kitchen on the 3rd floor of the Tsukiji Uogashi building — and the studio itself is not bookable by you. It is a commercial rental kitchen (¥66,000–176,000 per booking, email enquiries only) that hosts classes: the council's seminars, the festival programmes, third-party schools. The correct sentence, both halves: you cannot book the studio as a class, and the best classes in Tsukiji happen inside it. Reached by the external staircase and lift — indoors, seated, with a toilet, which is why this whole category is also the honest answer for rainy days, August heat, and anyone who can't stand in a lane for two hours.

Across the river, the Tokyo Metropolitan Government runs its own free layer at Toyosu: application-based behind-the-scenes tours (the August 2026 programme included the seafood building and its super-low-temperature freezer, applications by 9 August) plus walk-in activities at the "Ichiba no Hiroba" PR corner at Senkyaku Banrai, open 10:00–18:00 daily. All free, all Japanese, and heavily oversubscribed — effectively absent from English coverage.

A scope note in fairness to the paid layer: the December 2025 advisory in which the market asked operators to pause guided tours was a request scoped to that month, and it had not been reissued as of early August 2026 — but a class-only booking was never the target. It is the format the request implicitly recommends. More on that on visiting responsibly.

Before you book: season, kit, cash, sequencing

Book by season, not by day

  • The three festival weeks are the answer for free classes: late January, late April/early May, late October/early November. If your trip touches one and you can read a booking form, this layer is the best value in Tokyo food tourism.
  • August is the worst month for the free route: Fushitaka is blacked out 1 August–10 September, and the 2026 summer kids' sessions were marked full well before their dates. What August does carry is the TMG programme at Toyosu, above.
  • December is double-edged: the first fortnight has historically been the market's densest seminar run (katsuobushi, kombu, gizzard-shad curing and filleting sessions all ran 4–7 December 2025) — but Fushitaka is blacked out 1 December–5 January, and the market's own advisories have covered the second half of the month. Read 1–15 December as a genuine class window; 25–30 December as not a window at all.
  • Day of week: Saturdays for the tuna shows and festival classes; weekdays for Fushitaka, Aritsugu and the sushi schools; never Sundays or holidays for anything in the Uogashi Studio — or for Tamazushi.

What nothing supplies (and what everything does)

  • Bring to any hands-on market session: an apron, a cloth, a pen. Cool bags, ice packs and take-home packs are supplied — except at the alumni refresher, which asks you to bring your own.
  • Bring to the sharpening seminar: up to two of your own knives, wrapped in a knife case or newspaper/cardboard — the council's own instruction, and the practical legal standard for carrying a blade across Tokyo.
  • Bring to Fushitaka: your own kezuriki shaving box if you have one, so they can set the blade — the adjustment is the skill.
  • Bring to the commercial classes and Tamazushi: nothing. Tamazushi even lends the white coat and hat.

Paying, honestly

The cheapest things are the hardest to pay for. Council seminars go through Peatix (international cards, English checkout); the kids' extra-child supplement is cash on the day; Aritsugu's class is cash-in-hand at Toyosu, paid on the day; the commercial classes are normal card bookings in your own currency. Carry cash anyway — the nearest ATM that takes a foreign card is the Seven Bank machine in the information centre at Tsukiji 4-16-2, and there are none inside the Tsukiji Uogashi buildings. Details on the practical page.

The sequencing rule that makes a class day work

Shops shut first; classes are fixed. So walk the lanes at 09:00, be seated by 10:00 or 11:00, and come out at 13:00 into a market that is winding down anyway — do not plan to shop after a 13:00–15:00 seminar, because the dry-goods houses close between 12:00 and 14:00. A class doesn't save you time; it spends it in one place, and what it buys is a seat, a roof, a toilet and a skill. Budget 2–3 hours door to door for any seated class — and half a day if it's at Toyosu.
