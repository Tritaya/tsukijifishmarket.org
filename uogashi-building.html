The rulebook building · Tsukiji Uogashi 築地魚河岸 · verified 2 August 2026

Tsukiji Uogashi: the building with its own rulebook — open when the lanes aren't, and vice versa.

Two Chuo City buildings at the far end of the outer market run on a genuinely different clock: trading from 5:00 while the lanes are still asleep, serving soba and chicken rice upstairs until 18:00 after the lanes have shut — and closed every Sunday and public holiday even when the lanes outside are packed and trading. This page is the rulebook: the calendar trap, the 9:00 boundary, the no-eating ground floor, and the six 3rd-floor restaurants that each keep different hours.

TL;DR

  • This is not the lanes. Tsukiji Uogashi is a separate, city-built market hall with its own calendar and its own rules — it closes every Sunday and public holiday, even on days the outer-market lanes trade. In 2026 that bites twice: Wednesday 23 September and Sunday 27 December (derived — see chapter 03).
  • Come 9:00–11:00. The building opens at 5:00, but 5:00–9:00 is sales priority for trade buyers; nine o'clock is the official handover to general visitors, and stock is still complete. Fresh counters thin from about 14:00.
  • Never eat on the ground floor — prohibited outright, even food bought elsewhere. The eating happens on the 3rd floor: the six-restaurant Uogashi Shokudo food hall (all six open together only 10:00–15:00) and a free rooftop plaza with tables.
  • Free, unticketed, cash-first. No reservation for anything a normal visitor does; no ATMs inside, so bring cash.

What it is: two buildings, sixty counters, one public landlord

When the wholesale market left for Toyosu in October 2018, Chuo City had already built its answer: a purpose-built market hall to keep real fish trade on the Tsukiji side of the river. It pre-opened in November 2016 and grand-opened in October 2018, in step with the move. Two buildings stand where Namiyoke-dōri meets Harumi-dōri, at the far southwest end of the outer market: the Odawarabashi Building 小田原橋棟 (Tsukiji 6-26-1) and the Kaikōbashi Building 海幸橋棟 (Tsukiji 6-27-1), their rooftops joined by a bridge over Namiyoke-dōri.

The pedigree explains the rulebook. Construction ran July 2014 to March 2016 — three steel-frame storeys per building — and the opening slipped once, from 15 October to 19 November 2016. The owner is Chuo City; the operator is the city's Urban Development Public Corporation, which makes this effectively a public market with a landlord's calendar, not a shopping street's. That single fact is the root of everything below: the Sunday closures, the eating ban, the permit system. The wider story of what moved, what stayed and what the ex-market site is becoming lives on history & redevelopment.

3rd floor · Odawarabashi Building

Uogashi Shokudō 魚河岸食堂 — the food hall

Six restaurants, shared tables, wireless pagers, no queues worth the name — and Tsukiji's cheapest sit-down eating. Six different clocks, though: chapter 06.

Many fish counters will fillet (sanmai-oroshi) on request, and several carry pre-cut sashimi — which is what makes a trade-facing floor workable for a visitor who isn't cooking. If you are cooking in a Tokyo apartment, this building is the single best reason to come to Tsukiji at all. The ground floor was designed with wide central aisles (about 7 m of total circulation width), and an external staircase plus a see-through elevator run from the Harumi-dōri frontage straight up to level 3 — you can reach lunch without crossing the market floor.

How long to give it: 30–45 minutes walks both ground floors properly with a purchase; 60–90 minutes if you eat upstairs; two hours if you add the rooftop and treat it as the rest stop of a Tsukiji morning. The BBQ terrace is a booked 3-hour block on top. Comfort: the ground floor is roofed and rain-proof but not climate-controlled — the refrigerated cases keep the aisles cooler than the street, and it's genuinely cold at 7:00 in winter. The 3rd-floor food hall is enclosed and climate-controlled — the most comfortable indoor space in Tsukiji, and where to steer a family or an older companion when the unshaded lanes become unbearable. (Any “dress for sub-zero” advice you've read attaches to Toyosu's auction deck, not here.)

One honest routing note: these are trade counters, and the shopkeeper's expertise is the product. If you don't speak Japanese and want to actually buy something specific, a small guided market walk earns its keep here in a way it doesn't elsewhere in Tsukiji — the one we rate is a guide-fee-only walking tour (★ 4.8 · 2,006 reviews · ~$13, food paid as you go; read 31 October 2026). For everyone else, DIY: the building is free, covered, signposted and seated — there is nothing here a guide needs to unlock.

Who this building is actually for, persona by persona:

  • First-timer who wants to see a fish market and eat — DIY. Walk the lanes, arrive here at 9:30, eat on the 3rd floor. A tour adds cost and the group size the market keeps asking people to avoid.
  • Cooking in a Tokyo apartment or rental kitchen — DIY, and this is the building's strongest single use case. Ask the counter to fillet for you.
  • Non-Japanese speaker buying something specific — the one persona where a guide genuinely helps; make it a small private one, not a group walk.
  • Family, or a companion who can't stand in a lane for an hour — DIY, and route here deliberately: it's the sheltered, seated, toilet-equipped anchor of a Tsukiji morning.
  • Visiting in December — reconsider, or come to buy rather than to look (the advisory history is in chapter 03).
  • Wants the tuna auction — wrong site entirely. That's Toyosu.

The calendar trap: closed when the lanes are open

Four different clocks apply to this facility, and collapsing them is how visitors waste a trip:

  1. 05:00Building opens — trade buyers working, most tenants not yet fronting retail.
  2. 07:00The all-shops trading window begins (to 14:00). Upstairs, Senriken and Torito open at 7:00, Yabu Soba at 7:30.
  3. 09:00The handover: professional-priority window ends, the official visitor recommendation begins. Best two hours start now.
  4. 10:00All six food-hall restaurants finally overlap (until 15:00).
  5. 14:00Fresh counters begin shuttering — the BBQ terrace operator's own booking page warns some shops close from around 14:00.
  6. 15:00Ground floor done; building's stated close. Three restaurants trade on upstairs.
  7. 18:00Last orders at Torito; Yabu Soba and Toto Grill (weekdays) close around now.

The rule that catches people: Tsukiji Uogashi closes every Sunday and every public holiday — even when the outer-market lanes are trading. Its closed days are “Sundays, public holidays and designated Wednesdays”, following the wholesale-market calendar as a general rule; the Sunday/holiday closure is the facility's own stricter overlay. Cross-reading the official 2026 calendar against that rule, two days between August and December 2026 collide: Wednesday 23 September (Autumn Equinox — a public holiday the market specially trades on, having closed the 21st and 22nd) and Sunday 27 December (a specially opened trading day in the year-end rush). On both, expect the lanes open and this building shut.

Caveat, honestly held: those two dates are derived — the operator publishes the rule and the calendar separately, never the collision list, and “as a general rule” is exactly the phrasing that bends at year-end. Confirm against the year's own notices (the market association's calendar at tsukiji.or.jp, month view) before planning around either date. Designated Wednesdays vary month to month — check your specific week on the calendars page.

Two more calendar wrinkles. First, the December context: in December 2025 the outer market's NPO asked visitors — for the first time ever — to refrain from tourism visits during December, and asked operators to stand down guided tours; the request was December-only and had not been reissued as of 31 October 2026. If you're weighing a year-end shopping run against that history, come to buy, not to browse. Second, two sub-tenants keep their own calendars inside the building: the rooftop BBQ terrace opens on Sundays and holidays by full reservation even though the building's rule closes those days, and the Uogashi Studio runs its own booking grid (chapter 07).

The 9:00 boundary — and the floor you must never eat on

From 5:00 to 9:00 the ground floor is legally open to anyone, and nobody will stop you at the door. But that window is sales priority for trade buyers — the operator's own wording recommends general visitors and tourists come after 9:00. Pre-nine, you are walking through other people's working day: fine if you already know the place and can shop briskly without occupying a shopkeeper's attention, wrong if you're browsing with questions. Nine o'clock is the deliberate handover — and the practical sweet spot, because stock is still complete and the aisles are comfortable until the 11:00–13:00 visitor peak.

No eating on the ground floor. Ever. This is an outright prohibition, not a suggestion, and it explicitly covers food bought at surrounding shops — not just food bought inside. It is the single most-broken rule in the building. The sanctioned places to eat what you've bought are the rooftop plaza and the 3rd-floor rest space in the Odawarabashi Building, both seated. The walk-and-eat habit that (wrongly) attaches to the lanes outside is doubly wrong in here.

The rest of the rulebook, in descending order of how likely you are to break it:

  • Don't touch the goods. Pressing produce or prodding fish degrades saleable value — ask staff to handle anything you want inspected.
  • Don't haggle. Prices are wholesale-derived; negotiation is not a Tsukiji custom.
  • Ask before photographing a shop. The market-wide manner applies, and some counters post no-photography notices — a camera pointed at someone's till before 9:00 is the fastest way to be unwelcome.
  • Move in small numbers, leave the suitcase behind. Turret carts use the service lanes around the buildings; lockers and stroller storage (¥500) are at the Plat Tsukiji information centre a few minutes north — retrieve bags before it shuts (details: practical).
  • Cash. Ground-floor counters and food-hall tenants are cash-first, and there are no ATMs in the buildings. The rooftop BBQ terrace is the one card-friendly exception.
  • The 2nd floor is staff only — offices and storage, not a shortcut.
  • No smoking anywhere in either building; the outer market's only smoking area is behind Plat Tsukiji.

Filming anything serious? This building runs its own permit system — separate from the outer market's, and almost never reported in English. The sequence: get the target shop's consent first, then apply by PDF to the Tsukiji Uogashi Business Council (office on the Kaikōbashi Building's 2nd floor, desk hours 8:00–14:00, closed Sundays, holidays and market closed days), then collect a permit armband on the day and return it after. Shoots that don't name a specific shop are permitted only in the one hour between 12:00 and 13:00 on weekdays. Tripods are prohibited, as is any filming that restricts passage even briefly; allow about a week for processing. One first-hand account describes the ground floor as signed no-photography outright — consistent with shop-level bans, but not a published building-wide rule, so we won't state it as one. Wider market etiquette: visiting responsibly.

What the day actually feels like, hour by hour and day by day:

  • 5:00–9:00 — busy with trade buyers, almost no tourists. Dense with purpose, not with sightseers.
  • 9:00–11:00 — the sweet spot: fully stocked, comfortable, yours.
  • 11:00–13:00 — the busiest visitor window, matching the lanes' own peak outside.
  • 13:00–15:00 — thinning fast as fresh counters shutter; after 15:00 the ground floor is done.
  • Best days: Tuesday, Thursday, Friday. Saturday is open and fully stocked but is Tsukiji's busiest retail day — and one food-hall tenant (Toto Grill) drops to a 9:00–15:00 Saturday shift. Wednesdays: check that week's calendar. Sundays and holidays: never, for this building.
  • Late December (roughly the 25th–30th) is the single most congested period of the Tsukiji year — and the backdrop to the December 2025 advisory covered in chapter 03.

One thing this building is not: an auction venue. The tuna auction — and its lottery, and Sushi Dai's queues — are at Toyosu, 3 km east. Nothing at Tsukiji Uogashi requires a lottery, a ticket or a 4:30 alarm; that is precisely its charm.

What the counters actually sell

Around 60 shops split by trade: fresh fish, tuna, northern-seas products (salmon, ikura, kazunoko), shrimp, octopus, dried and salted fish, fish-paste products — plus produce, meat and eggs, which is what turns this from a photo stop into an actual shopping trip. Expect professional pack sizes rather than tourist portions, and remember the two money rules: cash (no ATMs inside), and no haggling. On tax-free shopping: assume no tax-free service at these counters — no per-shop registration list is published for this building, and nothing here is signposted as a registered tax-free shop. And from 1 November 2026 tax-free shopping anywhere in Japan is a refund rather than a discount: you pay the tax-inclusive price at the till, get customs confirmation at departure within 90 days of purchase, and receive the refund afterwards; the 5,000-yen minimum (before tax, per shop per day) stays, and the consumables cap and sealed-bag rules are gone. Buying knives or kitchenware to carry home? That's the knives & shopping page's territory.

The 3F food hall: six restaurants, six clocks

Uogashi Shokudō 魚河岸食堂, on the 3rd floor of the Odawarabashi Building, is a self-service food court: order and pay at the counter, take a wireless pager, carry your own tray. No storefront lists, no 90-minute lines — counter waits are minutes even at lunch, and the real midday constraint is finding a table (Odayasu has only 4 counter seats of its own; everyone shares the court's tables). Set meals run roughly ¥1,000–2,000 — the cheapest sit-down eating in Tsukiji — though no tenant publishes a current menu online, so treat every dish price you've read (including that range) as reported rather than posted.

The trap is the hours. The operator's page says the food hall runs 7:00–19:00 — but that's the outer envelope produced by a single tenant, and the city's own tourism listing says 7:00–15:00. What's actually true, from the official Japanese shop directory (verified 2 August 2026):

Read that table and three truths fall out. All six overlap only 10:00–15:00 — arrive by 14:00 if you want a free choice, because Tekka-ya's “no queue” can mean “no food” after that. After 15:00, three survive (Yabu Soba, Toto Grill on weekdays, Torito) — which is how the food hall outlasts the building's stated 15:00 close; the external staircase and elevator from Harumi-dōri make the 3rd floor reachable without the market floor, though no operator page actually explains the after-hours route, so phone ahead before building an evening around it. And don't trust the official English pages: the same site's English directory gives Torito as 9:00–16:00 against the Japanese page's 7:00–19:00 — a ten-hour divergence on the building's longest-trading restaurant (chapter 08). Where the two disagree, cite the Japanese.

A tenant-level footnote stale guides flatten: “did Tsukiji move or stay?” splits inside this food hall. Odayasu was founded in 1935 with the original market and moved its flagship to Toyosu in 2018 — this counter is the branch that stayed; Senriken (1914) likewise sent its original shop across the water. Eating here, you're at the Tsukiji end of businesses that now live on both sides of the 2018 divide.

And if your timing fails anyway:

  • Arrived after 14:00? Tekka-ya may be sold out and the fresh counters shuttering, but Yabu Soba, Toto Grill (weekdays) and Torito trade on to 18:00–19:00.
  • Building shut for a Sunday or holiday? The lanes outside may well be trading — check the calendar and pivot to the stalls.
  • Actually wanted the auction? Different site, different process, roughly two months' lead time for the deck lottery: the tuna auction page.

How this fits a Tsukiji morning: walk the lanes and their stalls at 8:00–9:30, work down to Uogashi by 10:00, buy on the ground floor, then eat upstairs before 14:00. If what you want is a proper sushi breakfast, that's a different page — and mostly a different clock.

The rooftop — and the ¥66,000 kitchen upstairs

The rooftops of both buildings are public space, joined by the bridge over Namiyoke-dōri: a wooden-deck plaza with tables, some roofed, looking back over the outer market. It is free, needs no booking, and is the sanctioned place to eat what you bought downstairs — first-hand accounts consistently describe the outdoor terrace as the reliably empty corner even when the food court inside is full. For families, this floor is the point of the whole building: seats, shade, a toilet (including an accessible one), a lift — the sheltered anchor the unshaded lanes cannot offer.

The facilities ledger, honestly kept:

  • Toilets: yes — including a multipurpose/accessible one, a genuine advantage in a district where public toilets are scarce. (Where exactly it sits, and whether both buildings have one, isn't published — only that the facility provides it.)
  • Seating: yes, everywhere upstairs — the food court's shared tables, the Odawarabashi event rest space, and the rooftop deck.
  • Step-free: yes — the see-through elevator runs street to 3F, and wheelchair entry is listed as possible; the catch outside is the crowded lanes, not steps.
  • Lockers: none inside — coin lockers up to suitcase size are at Plat Tsukiji, a few minutes north, which also stores strollers (¥500). Retrieve bags before it shuts — its counter closes at 14:00 (full hours: practical).
  • Wi-Fi, power outlets, ATMs: none in the buildings (Wi-Fi exists at Plat Tsukiji). Plan accordingly, especially the cash.

Two bookable operations live up here, and both are routinely misdescribed:

  • The rooftop BBQ terrace (Kaikōbashi Building 3F) — a separate commercial operator running a “buy it downstairs, grill it upstairs” format: regular pitch ¥19,250 for up to 10 people for 3 hours (about ¥1,925 a head full), sofa site ¥19,800 for up to 6, parasol table ¥3,630 per person, and a no-reservation standing-drink corner at ¥1,100 for up to an hour (last entry 15:30). Charcoal, equipment and clean-up included. Web bookings open exactly one month ahead and close 3 days out; Sundays, holidays and the evening plan are reservation-only — and it's the one place in the building that takes cards and QR payments.
  • Uogashi Studio 築地魚河岸スタジオ (Odawarabashi 3F) — not a tourist cooking class, despite what listings imply. It's a commercial rental kitchen: ¥66,000 tax-included per weekday half-day (¥132,000 full day; Saturdays ¥88,000/¥176,000), up to 32 seats, enquiries by email only. It's the venue that hosts classes — cooking schools and the market's own shopkeeper-taught seminars run 16–24 students in it. If you want a class you can actually book as a visitor, start at experiences & classes — or a sushi-making class near the market.

Myths this page exists to kill

“The food hall is open 7am–7pm, so eat there any time”

That's one tenant's envelope, not a promise. Only Torito reaches 19:00; three of six close at 15:00, and all six trade together only 10:00–15:00. Plan lunch by 14:00 — the board has each clock.

“Torito: open 9:00–16:00” — the official English site

The same website's Japanese page says 7:00–19:00 — a ten-hour divergence on one restaurant, plus a 7:00-vs-7:30 slip on Yabu Soba and closure days assigned to the wrong shops. When EN and JP official pages disagree at Tsukiji, the Japanese page wins — a pattern across this whole site, tracked on hours & calendars.

“Tsukiji Uogashi is just another name for the outer market”

Separate operation, separate landlord, separate calendar. This is a Chuo City facility with its own Sunday/holiday closure — which is why the lanes can be packed while its shutters are down (chapter 03). Guides that conflate the two get the closed days wrong both ways.

“Book the Tsukiji Uogashi Studio cooking class”

It's a kitchen for hire, not a class. ¥66,000 per weekday half-day, aimed at companies and cooking schools, email-only. Real bookable experiences: experiences & classes.
